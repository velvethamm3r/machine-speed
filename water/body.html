America's water fails most where people can least afford it.

The country has let its water systems run down, and when one gives out it is usually in a poor town. Every failure on this record that left residents without safe water happened in a community earning under three-quarters of the national median. The repair money and the rules have gone elsewhere. And the one threat that is still getting worse, cyberattacks on the plants themselves, has no federal rule at all. This page counts what that neglect has cost, and makes the case for paying to end it.

1What neglect looks like

Old pipes, small systems, no money

A water system doesn't fail on the day the taps run dry. It fails over decades, and the systems with the least money fall furthest behind.

Pipes past their useful life

20%

Roughly 450,000 miles of water main should already have been replaced. Fixing breaks costs about $2.6 billion a year. ASCE's grade: C−, same as in 2021.

ASCE 2025 Report Card

Most systems are small

81%

Of the country's roughly 50,000 community water systems serve fewer than 3,300 people. They account for most violations, because a town that small can't pay for the staff or the upgrades.

CRS R47315

Rates pay for repairs

17%

Of households served by the biggest utilities already can't afford their water bill. Repairs are paid for through rates, so the poorest towns are the ones that can't raise the money.

Patterson et al. 2023

Basic cyber requirements unmet

70%+

Of systems EPA inspected since September 2023 violated basic cybersecurity requirements. 97 large systems had critical or high-risk flaws.

2Who pays for it

The worst failures happened in the poorest places

The chart puts each incident on two scales: how much the affected community earns compared with the typical American household, and how bad things got for residents. The failures that made people sick or shut off safe water for weeks are all on the left side, in towns earning between a third and two-thirds of the national median.

American Water and California Water Service cover too many towns to place. Fifteen incidents can't prove a correlation on their own, which is why the national studies sit next to the chart. Hackers pick water plants by what's exposed to the internet, not by what the town earns.

4What it cost

Neglect costs more than any attack

Pick a measure. Flint, Jackson and Milwaukee come out on top of every one, and all three started with a decision to save money on treatment or upkeep. The cyberattacks hit utilities serving millions of people, but only one of them, Clayton County, actually cost residents safe water, and that was for a few hours.

5Where the money and the rules go

Rules come fast for security threats and slow for poor towns

Each bar starts at a disaster and ends at the first federal rule that was binding and addressed its cause. When Washington treated something as a national security problem, a rule came within weeks or months. When the problem was contaminated water, it took years, even after people died. Flint waited ten and a half years for a rule on lead pipes.

The clock on water cyberattacks started in February 2021 and is still running: 5.7 years, no binding rule.

6The threat that is climbing

Cyber is the one failure that keeps getting worse

Every incident gets a score for the worst thing residents went through. Contamination and broken infrastructure have already reached the top rung. Cyber started at the bottom and has climbed one rung at a time: the early attacks did nothing residents could notice, later ones forced plants to run by hand, then caused overflows and pressure drops, and in July 2026 one led to a boil-water advisory. None has made anyone sick yet. More than 70% of inspected systems still fail basic cyber requirements, and there is still no binding federal rule.

8What this adds up to

We can't keep ignoring this

The record on this page says four things.

The country has let its water systems run down. EPA puts the repair bill at $625 billion, a fifth of the pipe is already past its life, and 10,769 systems broke a health standard last year.

The people who pay for it are the people with the least. Every failure here that left residents without safe water happened in a community earning under three-quarters of the national median. The national studies say the same thing at scale.

The money and the rules go elsewhere. A pipeline attack got a binding federal rule in weeks. Flint waited ten and a half years. The federal repair fund was least likely to reach the small, poor, diverse towns whose water is worst, and most of what it sent was debt.

Cyber is the one that is still getting worse. Five and a half years after Oldsmar, attacks have climbed from no effect to a boil-water advisory, most systems still fail basic requirements, and there is no rule.

In 2010 the UN General Assembly voted 122 to 0 to recognize safe drinking water as a human right. The United States abstained. Sixteen years later, 28 million Americans can't afford their water bill and four million lead pipes still carry water into homes. Water security is not a luxury to fund when there is money left over. It is the thing the rest depends on, and it is time to pay for it.

UN General Assembly resolution 64/292, July 28, 2010: 122 in favor, none against, 41 abstentions. UN record · US statement

9How to read this page

What each number means

People served is the population of the system hit, not the number harmed. Days without safe water counts outages and boil-water or do-not-drink periods; Flint counts from the April 2014 switch to late 2016, when lead tested below the federal action level. Disclosed cost is what was published: a settlement, an aid package or a response bill. Lost-service value applies FEMA's $138 per person per day (2022 dollars) to people who lost drinkable water, and is an estimate, not a measured loss.

Limits

  • Costs are not comparable across incidents: Flint's is a legal settlement, Jackson's federal aid, Milwaukee's the cost of illness in 1993 dollars.
  • Flint's lead harm is not in the illness count: 6,000 to 12,000 children were exposed to lead.
  • Clayton County's advisory length was not published; its lost-service value assumes about six hours, the record's convention for “within hours.”
  • Town incomes are Census estimates for the city or county; poverty and race figures for the eight smallest towns are approximate. They describe the community, not the people who lost water. State incomes are rounded to the nearest $1,000.
  • The income chart is fifteen data points: a pattern, not a correlation you can put a number on. Poverty and race figures for eight small towns are approximate (incomes are checked), and hackers choose targets by what's reachable online, not by income. The evidence that poorer communities get more violations and slower fixes comes from the national studies cited next to the chart: Allaire 2018 and 2022, NRDC 2019, Mueller and Gasteyer 2021.
  • Milwaukee's income is today's; the city was poorer in 1993. For Puerto Rico, the island-wide figure stands in for PRASA's customers, who are about 97% of residents.
  • Legionnaires' clusters from building cooling towers, such as New York City's in July 2026, are excluded: the source was not the public water supply, and tap water stayed safe to drink.
  • Lead bands are read from EPA's published map; exact state counts are in EPA's 2025 service line projections.

Sources: EPA compliance report (CY2024), EPA Report on the Environment, Healthy People 2030, EPA 2025 DWINSA update, ASCE 2025 Report Card, CRS R47315, FEMA BCA, Corso et al. (CDC Emerging Infectious Diseases, 2003), and press and agency reports linked in each incident.
